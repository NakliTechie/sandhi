How stories work

Thirteen principles the masters keep rediscovering — each one drawn, then all of them used in a single story.

Sunday, 261004

A Greek philosopher, an Indian treatise on drama, a Russian playwright, an American novelist and the people behind Toy Story and South Park never sat in the same room. Yet when they wrote down what makes a story work, they kept arriving at the same handful of ideas.

This page lines those ideas up, draws each one, and then tells a short story that uses every one of them — so you can watch the machinery run.

The shape of it

Six shapes most stories reduce to

Reagan et al. (2016) ran sentiment analysis on 1,327 Project Gutenberg novels and found six core arcs. Drawn here as idealised shapes, not the measured curves.

The whistle is a Man in a hole. In the study, Icarus, Oedipus and Man-in-a-hole variants were among the most downloaded. The sample is English-language and mostly 19th-century, so treat it as one canon, not all storytelling.

Why it works — and what we left out

Brains fall into step

Princeton researchers scanned a storyteller and her listeners. The listeners’ brain activity tracked hers, the coupling vanished when they couldn’t understand her, and the closer the coupling, the more of the story they understood. Stephens, Silbert & Hasson, PNAS 2010.

Stories change minds

People more absorbed — “transported” — in a story are more persuaded by it (Green & Brock, 2000). A 2014 meta-analysis of 76 papers and over 21,000 participants found the effect holds, shifting beliefs, attitudes and intentions. That power cuts both ways: it works for liars too.

Guidelines, not rules

The famous “22 Pixar rules” were tips a Pixar story artist, Emma Coats, tweeted in 2011. Others labelled them Pixar’s rules; she later said she wished she’d called them guidelines. The Story Spine itself came from improv teacher Kenn Adams.

Where the masters disagree

Vonnegut says tell readers everything early; Stanton and Hemingway say make them work it out. Most Western advice says a story needs conflict; kishōtenketsu builds a whole form around a twist instead. When two masters disagree, both are tools, not laws.

Left out on purpose

Talks on storytelling often claim stories “release oxytocin” and make us trust. Studies have also linked oxytocin to envy, tribalism and less cooperation, and TED added a correction note to the best-known talk. The claim doesn’t survive scrutiny well enough to include.
